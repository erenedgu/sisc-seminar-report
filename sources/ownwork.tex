\section{Baseline Environment and Tools}
\label{sec:baseline-environment}

\subsection{Hardware}
\label{sec:hardware}

The numerical simulations were executed on the MBD high-performance compute server Gaia. Gaia cluster is equipped with 96 CPU cores, 1 TB of RAM, and 4 dedicated GPUs, providing the parallel processing capacity required to handle computationally intensive simulation workloads.

\subsection{Target Flood Solver}
\label{sec:flood-solver}

SynxFlow is an open-source Python package designed to simulate multi-hazard events, including flood inundation, landslide runouts, and debris flows \cite{Xia2025}. The flood solver relies on 2D shallow-water equations solved via Godunov-type shock-capturing finite volume methods \cite{Xia2017, Xia2025}. Crucially, SynxFlow addresses long-standing stability issues in shallow-water modeling through a Surface Reconstruction Method and an implicit friction discretization scheme \cite{Xia2017}. These improvements ensure the accurate calculation of slope source terms and maintain numerical stability even in the limit of disappearing water depths or across highly irregular topographies with abrupt slope changes \cite{Xia2017}. The software is divided into two distinct components: a high-performance C++/CUDA backend containing the physical solvers, and a Python Input/Output frontend that natively interfaces with standard libraries such as NumPy, Pandas, and Rasterio \cite{Xia2025, SynxFlowDocs2026}.

SynxFlow was chosen as the target hydrodynamic simulation framework for this study due to several advantages that align with the evaluation of energy telemetry. First, SynxFlow utilizes a hybrid execution model where both the CPU and GPU are active. The shallow-water equations are offloaded to CUDA-enabled GPUs, while the host CPU executes the Python IO interface to process data, manage boundaries, and orchestrate the simulation \cite{Xia2025}. This creates a complex and concurrent hardware usage profile that demonstrates the necessity of the Alumet telemetry framework. Furthermore, because this orchestration is entirely script-based rather than relying on traditional, low-level configuration files, it enables extensive workflow automation \cite{Xia2025}.

\subsection{Energy Telemetry Framework}
\label{sec:energy-telemetry-framework}

To overcome the challenges mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:limitations-related-work}, this study utilizes Alumet, a lightweight and modular telemetry framework designed for high-precision local and distributed measurements \cite{Alumet}. Alumet addresses the limitations of traditional hardware sensors by operating a measurement pipeline that simultaneously gathers data from both low-level hardware components (such as Intel RAPL for CPUs or NVML for NVIDIA GPUs) and operating system interfaces (such as procfs or cgroups for software metrics) \cite{Alumet}.

Rather than relying only on power readings that also capture background cluster noise, Alumet leverages the energy-attribution plugin \cite{Alumet}. This plugin combines the total hardware energy consumption data with the process-specific hardware utilization metrics \cite{Alumet}. By scaling the total component energy by the specific CPU or GPU utilization percentage of a target process, the framework calculates the energy consumed for the isolated process.



\section{Telemetry Integration and Experimental Workflow}
\label{sec:workflow}

SynxFlow and Alumet operate as separate software tools without interoperability. To bridge this gap, a custom Python-based framework was developed to combine the two tools into an automated experimental pipeline.

The workflow is structured around a configurable Monte Carlo loop. In each iteration, a distinct Gaussian noise perturbation is applied to the original DEM. Then the Synxflow solver is executed and energy profiling is done using Alumet, before accumulated telemetry and hazard data across the ensemble are mathematically post-processed.

\subsection{Single-Run Pipeline}
\label{sec:single-run}

The single-run pipeline in Figure \ref{fig:single_run_pipeline} represents the foundational layer of the framework. For each simulation run, the process begins by preparing a unique topographical landscape. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{images/single_Workflow.pdf}
    \caption{Single-run execution workflow}
    \label{fig:single_run_pipeline}
\end{figure}

Based on the standard deviation ($\sigma$) specified in the configuration file, Gaussian statistical uncertainty is injected into the baseline DEM. For every individual grid cell at spatial coordinates $(x, y)$, the perturbed elevation $Z_{\text{perturbed}}(x, y)$ is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
Z_{\text{perturbed}}(x, y) = Z_{\text{original}}(x, y) + \epsilon, \quad \text{where } \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
\label{eq:dem_perturbation}
\end{equation}

where $Z_{\text{original}}(x, y)$ represents the baseline elevation and $\epsilon$ is a zero-mean ($\mu = 0$) normally distributed random variable. To simplify the error model, the elevation noise is assumed to be spatially stationary and independent of both the absolute elevation ($Z$) and the local terrain gradient. Furthermore, the surveying equipment is assumed to introduce no directional or systematic spatial bias. Consequently, this produces a perturbed terrain raster that models isotropic measurement errors without introducing artificial elevation drift across the domain.

Once the perturbed DEM is generated, the orchestration script configures the physical environment for the flood simulation. The framework loads the perturbed DEM and establishes the spatial limits of the domain. It configures an open upstream boundary to inject a dynamic, time-varying water discharge (initiating at $1000,\text{m}^3/\text{s}$ and decreasing over the simulation period), alongside an open downstream boundary designed to maintain a constant water depth of 5 meters. With the help of Synxflow API, the environment can be customized based on the scenario's specific requirements, allowing the model to simulate continuous rainfall or apply spatially varying surface friction to represent different landcover types.

With the physical domain established, the orchestration framework finalizes the starting conditions by assigning initial water depths ($h_0$) and placing a spatial observation gauge to track water height over time. Once all environmental parameters are compiled, the simulation is dispatched. During this phase, Synxflow, as mentioned before, utilizes a heterogeneous hardware model. The host CPU orchestrates the data pipeline and input/output operations, while the computationally intensive 2D shallow-water equations are offloaded to CUDA-enabled GPUs for parallel processing.

Simultaneously, the Alumet framework operates as a background daemon to continuously capture the hardware's energy footprint. Dictated by the Alumet configuration file, the agent polls host operating system metrics and CPU registers every $50\,\text{ms}$ to measure processor utilization and power draw. Concurrently, it polls the NVIDIA GPUs every $200\,\text{ms}$ to log board-level power consumption and Streaming Multiprocessor utilization. The $50\,\text{ms}$ CPU polling interval balances power tracking against reading overhead, while $200\,\text{ms}$ was selected for GPU telemetry to stay within NVML query limits.

Crucially, rather than recording raw, unattributed node-level energy that includes background system noise, the framework employs a mathematical attribution model to isolate the simulation's exact footprint. For instance, the process-attributed GPU energy is dynamically computed at each time step as:

\begin{equation}
\text{Energy}_{\text{attributed, GPU}} = \text{Energy}_{\text{GPU, total}} \times \text{Usage}_{\text{GPU process}}
\label{eq:gpu_energy_attribution}
\end{equation}

This concurrent execution methodology ensures that the completion of every single run yields both the flood solver's results and process-isolated hardware energy profile.


\subsection{Ensemble Creation}
\label{sec:ensemble-orchestration}

While the single-run pipeline defines the core execution of an individual simulation, evaluating the impact of topographical uncertainty requires scaling this process into a probabilistic framework. To achieve this, an orchestration framework automates the single-iteration pipeline across a configurable Monte Carlo ensemble, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble_pipeline}. 

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{images/Workflow.pdf}
  \caption{Monte Carlo Pipeline Flowchart}
  \label{fig:ensemble_pipeline}
\end{figure}

To quantify uncertainty across the ensemble, the orchestrator establishes a target observation point to continuously record localized water depth time series. Before running the ensemble loop, a baseline map is generated to visually confirm the monitoring point locations.


To connect SynxFlow and Alumet, the orchestrator dispatches an execution loop that sequentially repeats the single-run pipeline, generating new terrain noise, running the GPU-accelerated solver, and capturing background energy telemetry for every iteration $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$. The end result is a multi-run dataset combining Synxflow outputs with process-isolated energy profiles, ready for ensemble post-processing.


\subsection{Post-Processing and Ensemble Analysis}
\label{sec:post-processing}

Following the completion of the automated ensemble loop, the post-processing script synthesizes the raw, asynchronous outputs from both SynxFlow and Alumet into synchronized statistical metrics and visual analyses.

To isolate SynxFlow's exact energy footprint from background operating system noise, the script performs dynamic Process ID (PID) tracking. Because Alumet logs performance metrics for the entire compute node, execution logs are parsed to identify the specific PID assigned to the SynxFlow solver during each iteration. Filtering the raw Alumet telemetry by this PID extracts only the process-attributed energy.

To resolve the sampling rate, where Alumet polls CPU metrics every $50\,\text{ms}$ while GPU metrics update every $200\,\text{ms}$, the script performs timeline synchronization. It constructs a master chronological timeline and linearly interpolates both energy streams onto this unified axis. Linear interpolation was selected over standard polynomial splines because it guarantees monotonicity in cumulative energy summation and avoids artificial overshooting between discrete sampling points. This ensures that cumulative CPU and GPU energy footprints are aligned with each other.

In parallel with hardware profiling, the script evaluates hazard uncertainty. Water depth time series recorded at the designated observation points are aggregated across all ensemble iterations. From these datasets, the script derives probability density distributions of localized water depths, quantifying how initial topographical noise translates into flood uncertainty.

Finally, the script computes ensemble statistics and generates analytical plots for cumulative energy over time, per-iteration energy costs, and energy distributions.
